Limitations Are Self-Imposed

october 18, 2025

I hate the phrase I can't.

What I disliked most about where I grew up was the limitations. There were boundary lines around what you were supposed to do and how you were supposed to be. We had a script to follow, and if you deviated, people looked at you like you'd ruined the whole play. I wanted to draw my own path instead of walking the outdated template that was already there.

For a while, I couldn't articulate why I love New York City so much. The answer came when I started living there for the second time—everyone drawn to NYC has the same aversion to limitation as me. The high variation between neighborhoods, the tall skyscrapers—they represent the lack of upper boundary. Everyone who lives there and gets it has one thing in common: they all love that there's no ceiling. You could move there for investment banking, pursue acting on the side, and DJ at Elsewhere on weekends.

Limits are optional. The only purpose they serve is to filter your system toward a specific result. I'm not condemning people who choose limitations—but there's a difference between filtered and constrained, and it depends on your intention. Ask yourself why you're introducing limitations: do you want to remove the noise, or do you want to minimize uncertainty? Removing noise comes from confidence in a particular goal—it's productive. Minimizing uncertainty comes from fear of failure or ambiguity. One sharpens focus, the other shrinks potential.

Limitations install structure, but innovation flourishes in unstructure. Structure is protection—it keeps things stable and predictable—but protection can turn into complacency. Our bodies work the same way: nerves create pain to warn us not to overextend, like when you flinch from something hot. But touching heat isn't always bad. It depends on how you perceive it. Fire can burn, but it can also forge, illuminate, transform. The difference is your relationship to the discomfort.

You can actually model this in optimization theory. Every time you say "I can't," you're adding a constraint to your objective function. Each constraint shrinks your feasible region—the set of all possible solutions. People think constraints make problems easier to solve, but they're really just limiting what answers you'll accept. The optimal solution to an unconstrained problem will always be at least as good as the constrained one.

Ultimately, your relationship with limits boils down to your objective function: are you using a boundary to remove the noise, or are you seeking to minimize your personal discomfort with uncertainty? One sharpens focus, while the other shrinks potential.

Are you comfortable coloring outside the lines?
